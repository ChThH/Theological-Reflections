*Thou hast been transfigured, O Savior, on Mount Tabor, indicating the transformation of mankind which shall take place at Thy dreadful Second Coming.*
*—From the Kathismata of the Transfiguration sung in Orthros*

The Greek word for transfiguration is metamorphosis; this is seen on the inscription of the icon. Both words mean a change in form. The transfiguration of Christ doesn't only show his divinity, but his humanity, humanity fully cooperating with divine grace. In Christ's humanity we who are united to him are restored to the divine image. This takes place throughout our Christian journey in this life and is completed at the dread second coming.    

But to better understand the transfiguration in Christ, let us first contrast it with the metamorphosis the world offers. Franz Kafka, in his short story *Metamorphosis* tells the tale of a modern man. In the course of his life, Gregor Samsa wakes up to find that he has become a loathsome insect. His family reacts rather negatively to this, increasing his isolation and despair. Even his sister who initally cares for him, comes to loath that she is doing so and she herself undergoes a negative metphorphosis. Through this story, Kafka relates that modern life, our broken jobs and relationships, transform us from humans into monstrous vermin. The culmination of this worldly metamorphosis is death and dehumanization, as shown on August 6, 1945 when the United States dropped the first nuclear weapon ushering us into the Atomic Age. The magnitude of this death and destruction shows us who the powers of the fallen world are, clearly illuminating St Paul's words that the powers behind the throne in this evil age are cosmic powers of darkness: the devils (Ephesians 6.11–13). 

But thanks be to God, Christ offers us a better way, for the faithful have been transferred from the domain of darkness into his own kingdom (Colossians 1.13). Whereas we once walked in darkness, enslaved to our passions and the devils, Christ raised us up with him in his Resurrection, seating us with him in the heavenly places in his Ascension, thereby creating us anew, showing us the path of righteousness and the good works to walk therein (Ephesians 2.1–10).
